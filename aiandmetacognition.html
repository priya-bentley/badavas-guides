Building Metacognition in the AI Era

Eleven research-grounded classroom strategies that help students plan, monitor and evaluate their own learning when AI can produce the work for them. Each runs in minutes, fits inside your existing content, and needs little or no new grading.

The foundation

What is metacognition?

Thinking about one's own thinking: knowing how you learn, and steering that learning while it happens (Flavell, 1979). Students do it in a repeating cycle.

PlanBefore the work: define the task, commit to their own thinking.
MonitorDuring the work: check what they actually understand.
EvaluateAfter the work: judge what they learned and what to change.
The 12 metacognitive skills students practice

Each process breaks down into specific skills students can practice. Select a skill to see the strategies that build it.

Task analysis, prior-knowledge activation and strategy selection are each built by a single strategy. If your course uses only a few strategies, include at least one from Plan so these skills are practiced.

Two parts: knowing and regulating

Research describes two connected parts. Knowledge of cognition is what a learner knows about themselves, tasks and strategies. Regulation of cognition is what a learner does with that knowledge: planning, monitoring and evaluating their work (Schraw & Dennison, 1994). Knowledge only improves learning when a student acts on it, and regulation only works when it draws on accurate knowledge.

Declarative knowledgeKnowing what: their strengths, weaknesses and what a task demands."I understand concepts quickly but struggle to apply them."
Procedural knowledgeKnowing how: the strategies available to them and how to carry them out."I can test myself instead of rereading."
Conditional knowledgeKnowing when and why: which strategy, or which kind of help, fits this task."This assignment is building my analysis, so I shouldn't hand the analysis to AI."

Conditional knowledge matters more than ever. When AI is one click away, every task now includes a decision about whether and how to use it, and that decision depends on knowing what the task is for.

What AI changes at each step

Plan: the AI's first response defines the task, prior knowledge is never activated, and "ask AI" replaces choosing a strategy.

Monitor: fluent text feels like understanding, trust in AI lowers checking, and students rely on the tool to tell them they're right.

Evaluate: a strong product hides weak learning, credit goes to the wrong source, and the reflection itself can be written by AI.

What a student learns in evaluating feeds the next round of planning. Regulation is a cycle, not a sequence (Zimmerman, 2002; Winne & Hadwin, 1998).

Why monitoring is the hinge

Students adjust what they do based on how well they think they are doing (Nelson & Narens, 1990). A student who believes they understand stops studying; a student who notices confusion slows down, rereads or asks for help. Monitoring links what students know about their learning to what they do next (Greene & Azevedo, 2007).

If a student's judgment of their own understanding is wrong, every decision that follows is wrong too: they stop too early, skip the hard part, or trust an answer they should have checked. Four of the eleven strategies target monitoring directly, and several others build it in as a step.

Why it matters now

Why does it matter now?

AI can support metacognition, replace it, or share it with the student, depending on how a course is designed (Dhurumraj & Labuschagne, 2026). Four shifts make deliberate design necessary.

The product no longer proves the thinking

Assignments have long assumed that producing the work required doing the thinking. AI breaks that link. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high school students, unrestricted GPT-4 access raised practice performance, but students scored 17% lower once access was removed than peers who never had it (Bastani et al., 2025). In a randomized study of essay writing, ChatGPT support improved essay scores without greater knowledge gain or transfer (Fan et al., 2025).

The monitoring signal gets distorted

Learners routinely mistake ease of reading for understanding (Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, 2013), and AI text is fluent by design. In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, higher confidence in AI was associated with less critical thinking, and higher self-confidence with more (Lee et al., 2025). AI weakens monitoring at exactly the point everything else depends on.

The usual fixes can be outsourced too

Reflection journals, learning logs and take-home self-assessments were built to make students think about their thinking. AI can now write them in seconds. Metacognitive practices have to be redesigned so the metacognitive act itself stays with the student.

AI widens existing gaps

AI tends to amplify the learning habits students already have: strong self-regulators use it strategically, while others offload more of their thinking (Dhurumraj & Labuschagne, 2026). Explicitly teaching metacognition benefits disadvantaged learners in particular (Education Endowment Foundation). Building it into course design is an equity decision.

Design principles

Six principles every strategy follows

The principles come from research on teaching metacognition effectively (Veenman et al., 2006; Education Endowment Foundation) and from what keeps metacognitive work with the student when AI is available. Use them to adapt a strategy or design your own.

01One named purpose

Every strategy targets one regulatory process, and both faculty and students know which one. A strategy with a vague purpose becomes busywork.

In practice: tell students "this is a monitoring check" rather than "this is a reflection activity."

02Embedded in course content

Metacognitive strategies transfer poorly when taught as generic study skills. They work when practiced on the actual material of the course.

In practice: run Brain First on this week's case, not on a separate study-skills worksheet.

03Students are told why

Students invest in metacognitive work when they understand the benefit. Explaining the purpose is part of effective instruction, not an optional extra.

In practice: one sentence on why, every time you introduce a strategy.

04Repeated, then faded

One-time activities rarely change habits. Practice runs across the term, and prompts get lighter as students take over the regulation themselves.

In practice: use the same strategy three or more times before expecting it to stick.

05The thinking stays with the student

Each strategy relies on something AI can't supply: it happens in the moment, concerns the student's own process, is low stakes, is spoken or social, or uses evidence only the student has.

In practice: move reflection into class time, or tie it to the student's own drafts.

06Students see the result of their judgment

Metacognitive judgments improve when students compare them with what actually happened. A prediction with no comparison builds nothing.

In practice: every rating, plan or attempt gets checked against a result.
