How to Build Integrity by Design, Not Hyper-Surveillance

Teaching and learning strategies for honest work in the age of AI

Ten research-based strategies for reducing the pull to misuse AI, building evidence of learning you can trust, and responding fairly when a concern comes up, without relying on detection software. Each one shows how strong its evidence is.

The approach

What is integrity by design?

Designing courses so honest work is the easiest and most worthwhile path, and responding to concerns with evidence and conversation rather than detection scores. It works in three moves.

Reduce the pullChange why students would take a shortcut.
Make learning visibleBuild evidence you can trust into the design.
Respond fairlyMeet concerns with evidence and conversation.
Why students cheat, and why most don't

Students deciding whether to cheat weigh three questions: What is my purpose? Can I do this? What will it cost me? (Murdock & Anderman, 2006). Lang (2013) names four classroom conditions that push the answers toward cheating: an emphasis on performance, high stakes, extrinsic motivation, and a low expectation of success.

In a survey of 14,086 Australian university students, contract cheating was associated with dissatisfaction with the teaching and learning environment, a perception of many opportunities to cheat, and speaking a language other than English at home (Bretag et al., 2019a). Most students don't cheat, and the reasons they give most often are their own morals and their motivation to learn (Rundle, Curtis & Clare, 2019).

The implication: most of what reduces cheating is ordinary good teaching, aimed at the reasons students have, not only the opportunities.

From catching cheating to trusting the evidence

AI use is now routine: in a 2025 UK survey, 88% of undergraduates said they had used generative AI for assessments (Freeman, 2025). Policing every task is no longer realistic.

Assessment researchers argue that the better question is validity: does this assessment show what a student can actually do? Cheating is one threat to validity among several, and some anti-cheating measures harm validity themselves, for example by excluding some students (Dawson et al., 2024). Australia's higher education regulator recommends security at meaningful points across a program, an emphasis on the process of learning, and authentic engagement with AI (Lodge et al., 2023).

How Bentley's policy fits

This guide offers teaching strategies; Bentley's Academic Integrity Policy and Procedures govern how suspected violations are handled. In brief, the policy asks faculty to:

  • State expectations for outside assistance in the syllabus or assignment guidelines.
  • Meet with the student first when a violation is suspected.
  • File an Academic Integrity Incident Report with the Director of Academic Integrity if they still believe a violation occurred. No sanction can be imposed without one.

Summary as of October 2026. Always check the current policy, and contact the Director of Academic Integrity with questions.

Why not hyper-surveillance

Why does hyper-surveillance fall short?

Detection software and heavy monitoring promise certainty. The research shows they don't deliver it, and they carry real costs for students and for learning. A few supervised checkpoints still have their place (Strategy 5).

AI detectors aren't reliable

A study of 14 detection tools found them "neither accurate nor reliable," and paraphrasing or obfuscating text made them worse (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023). Simple modifications cut six major detectors' accuracy by 17.4 percent, and the authors concluded detectors cannot currently be recommended for determining integrity violations (Perkins et al., 2024). OpenAI withdrew its own AI-text classifier in 2023, citing its low accuracy (OpenAI, 2023).

They misjudge some students more than others

Seven detectors flagged, on average, 61% of TOEFL essays written by non-native English writers as AI-generated, while classifying US eighth-graders' essays almost perfectly (Liang et al., 2023). A detector-led process puts multilingual students at greater risk of false accusation.

Monitoring has costs for learning

In a study of 631 students, higher test anxiety was linked to lower exam scores, especially for anxious students taking online-proctored exams (Woldeab & Brothen, 2019). Measures meant to stop cheating can also undermine an assessment's validity, for example by harming inclusion (Dawson et al., 2024).

Rules alone don't hold

Policies and AI-use scales that depend on students following instructions create an "enforcement illusion": a red "no AI" label, unlike a red traffic light, doesn't stop anything. Lasting change comes from structural changes to the assessment itself (Corbin, Dawson & Liu, 2025).

Design principles

Six principles behind every strategy

Use these to adapt a strategy to your course, or to check a practice you already use.

01Design for the honest majority

Most students don't cheat, and the main reasons they give are their own values and their motivation to learn (Rundle, Curtis & Clare, 2019).

In practice: build the course around the students who want to learn, not around the few who might not.

02Validity first

Ask what evidence of learning each assessment gives you, and treat cheating as one threat to that evidence among several (Dawson et al., 2024).

In practice: for each outcome, know where your trustworthy evidence comes from.

03Change the structure, not only the rules

Instructions students can ignore offer an enforcement illusion; changes to how the task works offer real assurance (Corbin, Dawson & Liu, 2025).

In practice: pair every AI rule with a design feature that makes the work visible.

04Reduce the reasons, not just the opportunities

Purpose, capability and cost drive students' choices (Murdock & Anderman, 2006), and performance pressure, high stakes and low expectations of success raise the pull (Lang, 2013).

In practice: before adding a control, ask which of the three questions it changes.

05Treat suspicion as a question, not a verdict

Detectors are unreliable and misjudge some students more than others (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2023).

In practice: a concern starts a conversation; Bentley's policy asks you to meet with the student first.

06Respond in ways that teach

An integrity strategy combines disciplinary and developmental responses (Bertram Gallant, 2008) and uses dialogue to rebuild trust (Sopcak & Stewart, 2021).

In practice: every case ends with the student learning something, and you adjusting the design.

The evidence

Why these strategies, and how strong the evidence is

Two strategies rest on strong evidence, five on moderate evidence, and three on expert consensus, Bentley policy or emerging work. We show the rating on every strategy so you can weigh it for your own course.

Organized around why students cheat

Students weigh their purpose, whether they can do the task, and what cheating will cost them (Murdock & Anderman, 2006). Strategies 1–4 each change one of those answers.

No reliance on detectors

Detectors are inaccurate, easy to evade, and misjudge non-native English writers (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023; Perkins et al., 2024; Liang et al., 2023).

Valid evidence, not policing every task

Validity matters more than cheating (Dawson et al., 2024), and rules alone create an enforcement illusion (Corbin, Dawson & Liu, 2025). So: secure a few points, open the rest.

How the ratings are defined

  • Strong: consistent findings across many studies or large samples.
  • Moderate: supported, but correlational, perception-based or small in effect.
  • Expert consensus: recommended by leading researchers and agencies, not yet trialed.
  • Policy + practice: required by Bentley policy and drawn from practice guidance.
  • Emerging: conceptually grounded, with limited direct evidence.

What the research can't tell us yet

  • Most cheating research predates generative AI; we use it where the mechanism carries over.
  • Design findings are mostly based on student perceptions, not measured cheating rates.
  • Effects are modest, so no single strategy is enough on its own.
  • Detector research ages quickly; we re-check it each year.
